Which wine should you drink from your cellar today?

By Omar Bariffi · Published 2 September 2026, updated 9 November 2026

The question is not only which wine suits dinner. It is which bottle in your cellar is ready, fits the occasion, and deserves to be opened now. A short, repeatable routine stops you from wasting important bottles and from letting others age past their best at the back of a rack.

Which bottles should I look at first?

Look first at the bottles that need attention: those in the ideal phase of their drinking window, those marked to drink soon, and those you have not checked for a long time. A useful cellar is not a static list, and narrowing the field to ten or fifteen candidates turns the choice into a quick decision rather than a chore.

The bottles people forget tend to be the same every time: single bottles received as gifts, wines hidden behind a case, whites bought for the summer and never opened, and odd formats such as halves or magnums. A simple white or rosé made to be drunk within two or three years of the vintage is usually the wine that suffers most from waiting.

  • Wines in the drink-soon or declining stage
  • Wines at their peak, if the occasion deserves them
  • Bottles with no notes, or not reviewed for over a year
  • Single bottles and gifts without a fixed place

How do I know a bottle is ready to open?

A bottle is ready when it sits inside its drinking window and recently opened bottles of the same wine confirm that the tannins have softened while the fruit is still alive. The window is a probable range, not an exact date: vintage, producer, format and storage can shift it by several years.

A few rules of thumb help. Barolo or Brunello di Montalcino from a classic vintage often starts to show well after eight to ten years and can last well beyond twenty. A straight Chianti Classico or a Barbera d'Asti is typically at its best between three and eight years. Good dry Riesling can develop for a long time, while most simple aromatic whites, such as an entry-level Gewürztraminer, are better young. Warm vintages tend to open earlier, cool ones later.

If you own several bottles of the same wine, the most reliable test is still to open one and write down what you find. With a single bottle, check the external signs: the fill level in the neck, a cork that is not seeping, and steady cool storage. No general guidance replaces the real condition of that particular bottle.

How do I pick the right wine for the food and the evening?

Choose the wine from what is on the plate and how much time you have, then check that one of your ready bottles fits. A ready wine is not automatically the right wine for tonight: mushroom risotto asks for something different from a barbecue, and an improvised Tuesday supper is not the moment for the last bottle of a great vintage.

Time matters more than people expect. Young Nebbiolo or Aglianico often improves with one or two hours in a decanter; if you only have twenty minutes before sitting down, choose something naturally open, such as Pinot Noir, Dolcetto or Valpolicella. An old wine, on the other hand, should be decanted only to leave the sediment behind, shortly before serving, with the bottle stood upright the day before: after twenty years the bouquet can fade quickly.

Serving temperature is part of the decision too. As a rough guide: sparkling wine at 6-8°C, light whites around 8-10°C, fuller whites 10-13°C, light reds 14-16°C, structured reds 16-18°C. A red at living-room temperature is often too warm; if it comes from a cellar at 13-14°C, half an hour on the table is sometimes all it needs.

How many bottles should I open for my guests?

For dinner, plan on roughly half a bottle per person, or one bottle for every two guests, plus one spare. A 75 cl bottle gives about six 125 ml glasses, so for six people three bottles is the baseline and four lets you relax.

With more guests, two or three bottles of the same wine, or of similar wines, usually work better than six different bottles: the conversation stays on the wine and nobody ends up with a single sip. Rare bottles and old vintages give more with four to six interested people than at a table of twelve. For a tasting, three or four wines in sequence with 50-60 ml pours let you open different bottles without excess.

If wine is left over, a young, structured red re-corked and kept in the fridge usually holds for two or three days; a similar white slightly less. Sparkling wine and very old wines are generally best finished the same evening.

  • Aperitif: one or two glasses each, often sparkling or a crisp white
  • Dinner: about half a bottle per person, plus one spare
  • Tasting: 50-60 ml per wine, four to six wines at most

What should I do with the last bottle of a wine?

Open the last bottle when your most recent tasting note says the wine is at its best, and choose an occasion and people who will appreciate it. Waiting for the perfect moment is the most common way to lose a great bottle.

If you bought a case of six or twelve, the last bottle is also the one that has waited longest, so reread your notes on the earlier ones. If the last note said "open, generous fruit, polished tannins", it is usually wise to drink it within a year or two; if it said "already a little tired", there is no reason to wait. Pair it with simple food that leaves room for the wine, and write down how it was: it becomes your reference if you buy the same producer in another vintage.

What should I open when nothing is ready yet?

When nothing is ready, open wines made to be drunk young, or the more approachable versions of the appellations you love, and let your long-ageing bottles rest. This is typical of cellars built in the last few years mostly from serious reds.

Concrete examples: Rosso di Montalcino instead of Brunello, Langhe Nebbiolo instead of Barolo, Beaujolais, Dolcetto, a white from Alto Adige or a Fendant from Valais. If you own several bottles of an important young wine, opening one to see where it stands is not a waste: with a long decant and rich food, such as braised beef or aged cheese, even a closed wine becomes readable.

Over time the fix is structural: keep part of the cellar, often a quarter to a third, in wines to drink over the next two or three years, so the bottles meant for ageing can actually age.

Leave a useful note for next time

After opening, record your impression, the pairing, how many people shared it, and the wine's stage of evolution. Two lines and a photo of the label are enough: tasting memory makes future choices more personal, because it tells you not just what should be ready in theory, but what you genuinely enjoyed and with which dish.
