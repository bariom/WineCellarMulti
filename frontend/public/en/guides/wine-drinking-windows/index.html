Wine drinking windows: when to open a bottle

By Omar Bariffi · Published 2 September 2026, updated 9 November 2026

A drinking window is not an absolute promise. It is a way to organise attention: what to hold, what to check, and what not to leave forgotten at the back of the cellar.

What is a wine's drinking window?

A drinking window is the span of years in which a wine should show at its best: developed enough to be enjoyable, but not so old that its fruit and structure have faded.

It is usually written as two years, for example 2027–2038, counted from the vintage. Before the window opens, a wine may be closed, with firm tannins or oak still in the foreground. After it closes, the wine is not necessarily undrinkable, but the fruit is more likely to have thinned out, leaving mainly acidity, alcohol and tertiary notes.

There is no single right moment within the range. Collectors who like fresh fruit tend to open early in the window; those looking for leather, forest floor, truffle and softened tannins prefer the later years. The window describes the wine, but the choice still depends on your own taste.

How long do the most common cellar wines usually last?

It depends mostly on style: reds with plenty of tannin and bright acidity are built to last for decades, while most whites and everyday reds are at their best in their first few years.

The ranges below are typical guidance and should be read with caution. Producer and vintage can move them considerably in either direction.

  • Barolo: often starts to open up around 8–10 years after the harvest and in classic vintages can hold for 25 years or more; more modern styles are sometimes approachable earlier.
  • Brunello di Montalcino: released after about five years, but generally at its best between 10 and 20 years; Riserva can go further.
  • Bordeaux cru classé: usually 10–30 years depending on the vintage and the château; lighter vintages are ready sooner.
  • Red Burgundy: village wines are often drunk between 3 and 8 years, premiers and grands crus roughly between 8 and 20, with wide variation by vintage and grower.
  • Merlot del Ticino: the standard bottlings are meant for the first 2–5 years; barrique-aged selections can hold for 8–12 years.
  • Champagne: non-vintage is usually best enjoyed within a few years of purchase; vintage Champagne can develop for 10–20 years.
  • Everyday whites: Pinot Grigio, Vermentino, Sauvignon Blanc and similar wines are best drunk within 1–3 years to keep their freshness.

What changes a bottle's drinking window?

Vintage, bottle format and storage conditions can bring maturity forward or push it back by several years, which is why a window should be read as a range rather than a deadline.

A warm, generous vintage often gives wines that are ready sooner, with softer tannins; a cool, austere vintage may need more patience but can reward it. Format matters too: in a magnum the ratio of wine to the oxygen in the neck is more favourable and development is slower, while a half bottle matures faster.

Storage is the factor you control directly. A cellar that stays steady at around 12–14 °C, in the dark, with some humidity and the bottles lying down, slows development. Temperature swings, a cupboard at 20 °C or a long summer in the boot of a car speed it up. Provenance counts as well: a wine bought at auction or passed through many hands may have a different history from a case delivered straight from the producer.

How do you use drinking-window stages to decide what to open?

Stages turn a list of dates into priorities: a young wine can wait, a wine at its peak is a candidate for the next occasion, and a wine marked to drink soon needs a concrete decision.

In practice, looking at your cellar by stage is more useful than remembering individual dates. If you own six bottles of the same wine, you can open one as the window begins to see where it stands, then spread the rest over the following years. If a whole case moves into the drink-soon stage, it is better to plan for it now than to discover it in five years.

  • Too young: let it rest, unless you want a test bottle out of curiosity.
  • Opening up: good if you enjoy primary fruit, and useful for checking how the wine is developing.
  • At its peak: a candidate for an important dinner or a guest who will appreciate it.
  • Drink soon: schedule it for the coming weeks or months.
  • Past the window: open it without high expectations, ideally with people who enjoy mature wines.

How can you tell a wine is past its best?

A wine is past its best when the fruit has faded and what remains is mostly acidity, alcohol and oxidative notes, without the depth the bottle once had.

Visual signs help: a red that has turned clearly brick or orange even at the core of the glass, or a white that is deep gold or amber when it should still be pale straw. On the nose you may find stewed fruit, walnut, or sherry and Madeira notes where they do not belong. On the palate the wine is short and feels dry and hollow. Before the bottle even leaves the rack, a fill level that has dropped below the neck, a cork pushing upwards or stains down the capsule are warnings worth taking seriously.

There are edge cases, though. Some wines go through a closed phase: a Barolo or a Bordeaux at 6–8 years can seem mute and then open up again later. And cork taint is a fault of the individual bottle, not a sign of age: another bottle of the same wine may be perfect.

Calibrate with your own cellar experience

Temperature, humidity, transport and cork condition all matter, and no window can know them for you. Tasting notes from bottles you have already opened are the most reliable way to adjust the guidance: if the last bottle from a case was already quite developed, bring the others forward; if it was still tight, wait.
